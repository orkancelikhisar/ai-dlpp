Is the browser the bottleneck? — AI-DLPP capability-ceiling study, executive summary

Policy-conditioned, fully-local detection of confidential data in LLM prompts · shippable in-browser 2–4 B models vs the same method on hosted open-weight models of 27–120 B where the size is published · 6,502 calls, three passes at temperature 0, every figure regenerated from artifacts · draft v1, 8 Sep 2026

Question. Our in-browser detectors find the right region of a prompt and attach the wrong label. Is that the browser's constraint, or the task's difficulty? Method. Run the identical compiled judge and policy-in-context baseline on six much larger hosted models, providers pinned, three passes, and print a model-free floor beside every number.

The headline, three ways. Only the metric changes between A and B — same rows, same gold. Scored span-wise the hosted arms tie a regular expression; scored on the message-level judgment the annotators actually gave, they clear it while the in-browser arms stay far below. In C the label-informed oracle (told how many spans to find) is beaten only by the reasoning model.

Why the in-browser arms fail: precision. They reach recall only by firing on most messages (P ≤ 0.25); the hosted judge separates on precision at the same recall.

The thinking-off ceiling is a ceiling on that inference mode, not the models. With reasoning allowed to finish, GLM goes from 0.042 below the floor to 0.186 above it, and its policy-in-context arm is the only one to beat the label-informed span oracle (0.809 vs 0.633 on the rows it answered).

What transfers to a local server: token counts. The compiled judge is 2.1–3.7× faster and 2.05× cheaper than policy-in-context on the same model because it answers in 5–7 tokens; that gap is prompt size and does not shrink with hardware.

A single pass against a hosted provider is not a measurement. This study's first headline came from one pass, one metric and one floor — each the least favourable available — and reversed when the repeat passes landed.

Conclusion

  1. The browser constraint is a real part of what costs accuracy. On the judgment the gold records, models 6–60× larger than the shippable ones go from far below a trivial floor to clearly and repeatably above it (+0.105 mean over three passes); on the stricter span-matched reading they tie it. Both are true; the original "scale stops at a regex" rested on the stricter reading alone.
  2. The binding constraint at every scale is classification, not span extraction. Spans are placed byte-exactly by a 2 B model and resolved cleanly 99.2% of the time at 27–120 B; what moves between passes, and what separates arms, is the label.
  3. Carry the compiled judge to a local server, alongside policy-in-context for entities. The judge wins the semantic predicate (0.927 vs 0.457) and is cheaper and faster; policy-in-context wins entity finding (0.855 vs an always-fire floor of 0.727). A deployment runs both — which the tiered design already does.
  4. Reasoning buys accuracy at a latency price this corpus makes visible — 31.8 minutes for 189 messages on the B family — and at 600 tokens the token cap, not the model, was the limit.
Verdict: the task is learnable by open-weight models a company could host; the shippable in-browser tier is not yet capable of it; the gap is precision on the semantic predicate, and it is substantially the price of the in-browser constraint.

Bounds: one policy (p-fin), one predicate, 19 gold positives (one item ≈ 0.03 F1 for the headline arm); the corpus's known generator leak gives the capitalisation floor perfect recall, so no floor figure transfers to a corpus without it; the thinking-ON result is one model, one pass, with 31% of rows lost to provider rate limiting; hosted quantisation is the provider's.
